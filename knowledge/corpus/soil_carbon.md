Soil organic carbon (SOC) is usually reported as a fertility indicator, but functionally it behaves as the habitat variable for the soil food web. Carbon is the energy source for heterotrophic microbes, and the organic fraction is also what binds mineral particles into stable aggregates. Those aggregates create the pore network that determines how much water the soil holds and how much oxygen reaches microbial habitat at depth.

Typical interpretation bands for cultivated topsoil in tropical and sub-tropical systems:

- below 0.5 percent: severely depleted; microbial biomass and aggregate stability are both limiting, and yield response to fertiliser is poor because water and structure are the binding constraints
- 0.5 to 0.75 percent: depleted but responsive; organic inputs give the fastest measurable gain
- 0.75 to 1.5 percent: moderate; typical of well-managed cropland with residue retention
- above 1.5 percent: good for cropland in these climates

SOC accumulation is slow and asymptotic. A depleted soil under improved management typically gains on the order of 0.1 to 0.5 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year in the surface layer, with the rate declining as the soil approaches a new equilibrium set by climate, clay content and input rate. Gains are reversible: a single season of inversion tillage or residue burning can release a large share of several years of accumulation.

Two implications matter for recommendation systems. First, any promised SOC gain must carry a time horizon, because the same intervention is negligible at one year and substantial at five. Second, SOC change is a lagging indicator; microbial biomass carbon and aggregate stability respond within one to two seasons and are better short-term monitoring proxies.
