Land use change is identified as the largest single driver of terrestrial biodiversity loss globally, ahead of climate change, pollution and invasive species at present. The mechanism operates through two distinct pathways that need to be separated when reasoning about a specific site.

The first is habitat loss: total area of suitable habitat declines, and species richness follows an approximately power-law relationship with area, so proportionally large losses of richness occur once remaining habitat falls below roughly 10 to 30 percent of the original extent.

The second is fragmentation: the same total area divided into smaller, more isolated patches supports fewer species than one continuous patch, because small patches have proportionally more edge, cannot support species with large home ranges, and experience local extinctions that are not replaced by immigration when patches are isolated.

The practical consequence is that connectivity interventions can deliver biodiversity gain without any increase in habitat area. Linear features - hedgerows, riparian strips, shelterbelts, road verges, field bunds - function as corridors and as habitat in their own right. Because agricultural landscapes are already criss-crossed by such linear features, converting them from bare or monoculture to structurally diverse native vegetation is generally the lowest-cost connectivity gain available.

Landscape heterogeneity matters independently of patch quality. A mosaic of crop types, field sizes, and non-crop elements supports more species than a uniform landscape of the same average quality, because species differ in which element they use and when. This is why crop diversification registers as a biodiversity intervention and not only an agronomic one.
