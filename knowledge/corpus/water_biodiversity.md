In arid and semi-arid systems, annual rainfall totals are a weak predictor of both crop performance and faunal diversity. What matters is the distribution: rainfall arrives in a small number of high-intensity events, and the fraction that becomes plant-available water depends on infiltration capacity at the moment of the storm. A degraded, sealed surface can shed most of a heavy event as runoff while a structured soil under the same rainfall stores it.

This produces a coupling that single-variable advice misses. Soil organic carbon raises aggregate stability, which raises infiltration, which converts the same rainfall into a longer period of plant-available moisture. Longer soil moisture persistence extends the growing period, which extends the period during which vegetation provides forage, cover and humidity for invertebrates and the vertebrates that depend on them.

Standing water is typically the scarcest habitat type in dryland agricultural landscapes, and its scarcity is disproportionate to its area. Amphibians require it for breeding, many bird species require it for drinking within a constrained daily range, and aquatic invertebrate assemblages are entirely dependent on it. Small permanent or semi-permanent water bodies therefore function as stepping stones whose biodiversity value per hectare far exceeds that of the surrounding cropland.

Temperature interacts with water through evaporative demand. Warming raises potential evapotranspiration, so the same rainfall supports a shorter effective growing period than it did historically. Interventions that reduce evaporative demand - shade, windbreaks, mulch, canopy cover - therefore act as adaptation measures even when rainfall itself is unchanged.
