A large share of global food crop volume depends to some degree on animal pollination, and the dependent crops are disproportionately the nutrient-dense ones - fruits, vegetables, nuts and pulses - rather than the staple cereals. Pollinator decline therefore registers as a nutrition risk before it registers as a calorie risk.

Two resource constraints are consistently identified and are often confused with each other.

Floral continuity: a crop in bloom for two to four weeks cannot sustain a pollinator population across a year. Populations are limited by the periods when no forage is available, not by the peak. Interventions that add flowering resources outside the crop bloom window therefore produce disproportionate gains relative to interventions that add flowers during it.

Nesting resources: most wild bee species nest either in bare, undisturbed, well-drained soil or in cavities and pithy stems. In intensively cultivated landscapes both are scarce, because tillage destroys ground nests annually and tidy field margins remove stem and cavity resources. Adding forage without adding nesting sites produces transient visitation rather than resident populations.

Wild pollinator diversity provides functional redundancy that managed honeybees alone do not. Species differ in their temperature, wind and light thresholds for foraging, so a diverse assemblage maintains pollination service across a wider range of weather conditions. Under poor bloom-period weather, crops served by diverse wild assemblages show more stable fruit set than those relying on a single managed species.

Pesticide exposure, particularly to broad-spectrum insecticides applied during bloom, is a well-supported contributor to mortality and to sub-lethal effects on foraging behaviour and navigation.
