Species richness is the count of species present and is the most widely used biodiversity indicator, but it is insensitive to composition. A site that loses ten specialist species and gains ten generalists shows no change in richness while having undergone a substantial functional loss. Richness should therefore be paired with a composition measure or with an indicator group whose specialists are known.

Shannon and Simpson diversity indices incorporate relative abundance and so detect the dominance shifts that richness misses. A falling Shannon index with stable richness is the classic signature of a system being taken over by a few tolerant species.

Habitat diversity, measured as the number and interspersion of distinct habitat elements in a landscape, predicts species richness across taxa better than the quality of any single element. This is the quantitative basis for recommending mosaic and heterogeneity interventions.

Useful indicator groups for agricultural monitoring, chosen because they respond fast and are cheap to survey:

- earthworm counts and microbial biomass carbon for soil condition, responding within one to two seasons
- ground beetle and spider activity-density for chemical load and structural refuge
- wild bee morphospecies counts along a fixed transect for floral and nesting resources
- bird point counts for landscape structure and woody cover, responding over several years
- aquatic macroinvertebrate family scores for water quality in drains and streams

Response times differ by an order of magnitude across these groups. Microbial and invertebrate indicators move within a season or two, plant community composition over several years, and bird and mammal assemblages over five to fifteen years. Any monitoring plan attached to a recommendation should state which indicator is expected to move first, or it will appear to have failed when it has simply not been given time.
