Nutrient loading. Nitrogen and phosphorus applied in excess of crop uptake do not remain in the field. Nitrate is mobile and leaches to groundwater or runs off to surface water, where it drives algal growth, oxygen depletion and loss of sensitive aquatic taxa. The loss is driven by the mismatch between application timing and crop demand as much as by the total dose, which is why split application improves efficiency at unchanged or reduced total rates. Elevated soil phosphorus additionally suppresses the arbuscular mycorrhizal symbiosis, so high-input systems lose a below-ground function they then have to replace with more input.

Pesticides. Broad-spectrum insecticides applied on a calendar schedule suppress pest and natural enemy populations together. Natural enemies typically recover more slowly because their populations are smaller and their generation times longer, producing pest resurgence and secondary pest outbreaks. Threshold-based spraying, mode-of-action rotation and avoidance of broad-spectrum products during bloom address this without abandoning chemical control.

Residue burning. Burning removes the carbon input that would otherwise build soil organic matter, kills surface-dwelling fauna directly, and emits particulate matter and greenhouse gases. It is usually adopted for speed of field turnaround rather than agronomic benefit, so the substitute must solve the turnaround problem, typically through zero-till seeding into standing residue.

Deforestation and land conversion. Beyond carbon stock loss, conversion removes the structural complexity that supports multi-layer faunal assemblages. Replacement by monoculture plantation restores canopy cover statistics without restoring species assemblages, which is why tree cover percentage is a poor standalone biodiversity indicator and must be read alongside species composition.
